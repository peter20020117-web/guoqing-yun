Balance

Water of Leith

The final work depicts a conscious effort to regulate attention and inner emotions while walking along the Water of Leith. It is the piece that most directly emulates a traditional Minimalist compositional approach, using pitched percussion to represent inner fluctuations and changes in rhythmic organisation to suggest the gradual emergence of peace. At the same time, the orchestration reflects aspects of the natural acoustic environment of the river.

The main musical body uses four instruments: vibraphone, xylophone, marimba and the synthesizer sound “Glow.” Across three sections, these materials undergo distinct transformations. In the first section, all four instruments continuously and randomly repeat their motifs, each following a different repetition pattern, creating a unified but constantly changing texture. Moments of silence in the xylophone and marimba prevent excessive note density and auditory fatigue, while the overall disorder reflects the complex and unsettled activity of the mind.

At around two minutes, the second section begins. The instruments continue their individual repetitions, but the repeated materials change, and the marimba and “Glow” begin to respond to harmonic shifts. These harmonic changes represent a gradual return to order as I become increasingly able to control the direction of my emotions and attention. At around three minutes, the third section introduces a new synthesised timbre. Together with the vibraphone, it changes from random repetition to a regular ascending scale, while the xylophone and marimba retain their irregular repetitions. This creates a coexistence of regularity and instability rather than eliminating the latter completely.

The third section contains richer chord progressions, with all instruments following the harmonic development while maintaining their own rhythms. This expresses a state in which calmness and restlessness can coexist, and in which anxiety is no longer completely controlling the inner state. Additional pad and rhythmic synthesizer sounds enrich the listening experience, while the use of only two virtual instrument sounds helps maintain a unified sonic character. Unlike the first two pieces, where environmental recordings form an integral part of the musical material, the Water of Leith recordings here mainly establish spatial context: flowing water, wind moving through trees and the voices of passersby are processed with simple fades in and out.

For the Dolby Atmos mix, the environmental sounds were arranged according to their natural spatial positions: water sounds were placed at the front and below, while wind and other ambient sounds were positioned toward the rear and above. In the first section, the pitched instruments were distributed across different positions and automated through space, while small percussion sounds also received spatial automation to enhance sonic movement. Sustained metallic tones were positioned high and automated through the sound field, with a “Size” value of 54 to increase surround extension. In the third section, the pitched instruments remain stationary to communicate stability and tranquillity, while the sustained metallic tones continue moving through automation, providing spatial variation without disturbing the sense of balance.
