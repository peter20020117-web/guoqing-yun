Cacophony

Edinburgh city centre · Royal Mile

The first work captures the inner anxiety, restlessness and distraction that precede the restorative experience. It was developed from my first visit to the Royal Mile in Edinburgh city centre during the arts festival, when the streets were crowded and my attention was constantly drawn to street performers and vendors. Although the visit was exciting, returning home brought a contrasting sense of disorientation, loneliness and silence, accompanied by a sudden awareness of anxiety. The title “Cacophony” therefore refers both to the clamour of the city centre and to the inner turmoil it provoked.

The main sonic material is field recording captured on a Zoom H6 on the Royal Mile. I processed the recordings with GRM Tools’ CombFilter and Shuffling. CombFilter introduced a pronounced sense of pitch and resonant tonal qualities, while Shuffling fragmented these pitch-resonant sounds and randomly reassembled them, also broadening the soundstage. The resulting material became a series of rapid, repetitive pulses. I then re-edited the original recordings, interweaving strong wind and street-performance material to shape the overall form.

In terms of compositional approach, I initially tried to draw on the density and complexity associated with traditional Minimalist music, particularly the work of Terry Riley. In practice, I found that the original instrumental approach did not generate enough tension and instead sounded too subdued, partly because instruments such as marimba introduced a more natural character. This experience informed the later orchestration of Balance. Across the project, repetition, sustain, limited material and gradual change are used as methods of auditory organisation rather than as an attempt to reproduce Minimalism as a strict historical style.

For the Dolby Atmos mix, the vocals and electric-drill sound were spatially automated, with the drill positioned below the listening position and the vocals above it. Processed and unprocessed ambient sounds were placed to the sides and behind the listener, with a substantial “Size” value used to expand their spatial presence. The first synthesizer sound was fixed in the lower-centre rear, while two other synthesizer sounds moved through the space by automation. The closing noise was automated from front to back, creating a sense of physically moving through the noise.
